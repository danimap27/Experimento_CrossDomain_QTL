\begin{abstract}
Sequential training of variational quantum classifiers produces interference
between tasks, a failure mode known as catastrophic forgetting. Memory-based
remedies conflict with the resource constraints of near-term quantum devices,
which motivates initialization strategies that leave no memory footprint after
training. This study characterizes one such strategy, where a hybrid
classical-quantum model is pre-trained on a synthetic Gaussian source that
shares no semantic content with the target sequence, and the resulting
parameters initialize the sequential phase. A controlled factorial re-run of
360 training runs separates the contribution of the initialization from that
of the optimization regime, and a benchmark campaign of 290 further runs
extends the evaluation to class-incremental sequences of up to five tasks,
qubit counts from four to eight, circuit depths from two to four and data
budgets from five hundred to twelve thousand samples, under an ideal
simulation and a calibrated device noise model, with paired tests, effect
sizes and bootstrap intervals throughout. The controlled evidence relocates
the effect that the original protocol attributed to the initialization. The
apparent reduction of the forgetting drop is carried by the learning-rate
regime (about five points, $p<0.001$), the synthetic initialization produces
no significant change under matched schedules, and the null reading is
stable across benchmarks, scales and budgets. Mechanism probes show that the
prior places the circuit in a far more active gradient region (roughly three
orders of magnitude in the first layer) without a retention gain, so
gradient signal and continual-learning performance dissociate and the
separation between arms lies in the starting point rather than in a loss
barrier. Rehearsal is the only evaluated remedy that improves average
accuracy on the standard class-incremental benchmarks ($+15.5$ points,
$p<0.001$), at a quantified cost in plasticity, and synaptic intelligence
is the only weight-regularization baseline that improves over the plain
model ($+4.1$ points, $p=0.007$) while cutting forgetting by an order of
magnitude. The results are framed as a
controlled characterization of cross-domain pre-training for quantum
continual learning, not as a new method, and the limitations of the current
scale are stated explicitly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ======================================================================

Variational quantum circuits have become the workhorse of quantum machine
learning in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum regime, where the reliable
circuit size is limited by gate and readout errors \cite{Preskill18,
cerezo2021variational}. A variational circuit encodes classical data into a
quantum state and updates rotation angles through a classical optimizer
\cite{Mitarai18, biamonte2017quantum}, which makes the resulting
model a candidate for classification tasks in settings where the data
arrives as a stream rather than as a single static dataset
\cite{RODRIGUEZ-DIAZ26}. When such a circuit is trained on a sequence of
tasks without access to previous data, the updates for a new task overwrite
the parameters that encoded earlier tasks, and the accuracy on the earlier
tasks collapses. This phenomenon, catastrophic forgetting, has been
characterized in classical networks \cite{McCloskey89,
parisi2019continual} and has recently been observed on superconducting
hardware for quantum classifiers \cite{Zhang26}.

These limits have motivated two families of remedies in the classical
literature. Regularization-based methods penalise changes to parameters that
were important for previous tasks, with elastic weight consolidation as the
canonical example \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} and with alternatives
that estimate importance online \cite{Zenke17}. Replay-based
methods interleave stored samples or stored predictions with the current
task, from plain experience replay \cite{lopezpaz2017gem} to the
logit-distillation variants that preserve the soft decision boundaries of
previous tasks \cite{buzzega2020dark}. Both families carry costs that sit
badly with the constraints of near-term quantum devices. Regularization
requires a Fisher information estimate whose storage and computation grow
with the number of parameters, and replay requires a memory buffer whose
size grows with the sequence \cite{mcclean2018barren, Wang21}. Recent
work has shown that replay can be transferred to quantum-classical heads
without destabilising training \cite{martinperez2026derpp}, which raises the
question of what can be done when even a small buffer is not affordable.

In parallel, quantum transfer learning has developed the machinery to reuse
knowledge across domains: a frozen classical network extracts compact
features and a variational circuit is fine-tuned on them, which allows small
quantum circuits to operate on high-dimensional inputs \cite{mari2020transfer,
martinperez2026cmes, martinperez2026soco}. The transfer-learning literature
in the classical setting has long recognised that pre-training on data
unrelated to the target domain still produces features that transfer well
\cite{Yosinski14, pan2009survey}. Whether the same logic holds
for a variational circuit in a sequential setting, and what property of the
resulting initialization protects later tasks, remains open. The observation
that layerwise initialization strategies stabilise the training of quantum
circuits \cite{Skolik21} indicates that the starting point of a
variational circuit is not a neutral design choice.

These constraints frame the question of this study: does pre-training a
hybrid quantum-classical classifier on a synthetic source that shares no
semantic content with the target tasks produce an initialization that
reduces forgetting during later sequential training, and under which
conditions does the effect hold? The study answers the question for a
four-qubit hybrid model across circuit topologies, noise profiles, data
budgets and qubit counts, with a statistical protocol built on paired
comparisons over seeds.
The contributions are the following. First, the cross-domain synthetic
pre-training setup is formalised for a sequential quantum classifier, with
the source, the task sequence and the evaluation protocol stated precisely.
Second, a controlled factorial design separates the effect of the
initialization from the effect of the optimization regime and shows that
the apparent reduction of the forgetting drop, of about six points under
the original protocol, is carried by the learning rate, while the
initialization itself produces no significant change once rates are
matched. Third, the null reading of the initialization is shown to be
stable across class-incremental benchmarks of up to five tasks, data
budgets from five hundred to twelve thousand samples and scaling grids
over qubit counts and depths, and the comparative evaluation is reported
throughout with absolute accuracies, paired tests, effect sizes and
confidence intervals, which corrects two reporting defects of the earlier
conference version of this study, namely the omission of absolute
accuracies and a mislabeled retention table. Fourth, mechanism evidence
based on gradient statistics, curvature and loss interpolation locates the
role of the prior in the geometry of the starting point rather than in
retention, which bounds what initialization alone can deliver and
redirects attention to the components that do act, such as rehearsal and
synaptic intelligence, whose behavior under the same protocol is quantified. The rest of the
manuscript is organized as
follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the related literature.
Section~\ref{sec:background} fixes the scenario definitions and the metrics.
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the pre-training methodology, the
circuit architectures and the sequential protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their scope and limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Continual learning in classical networks}

The sequential-learning problem was formalised by
McCloskey and Cohen \cite{McCloskey89} and has since produced
a taxonomy of strategies that spans regularization, replay and architectural
design \cite{parisi2019continual, Lange22, Wang24}. Elastic weight
consolidation introduces a quadratic penalty weighted by the diagonal of the
Fisher information matrix \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}, and synaptic
intelligence replaces the Fisher estimate with an online accumulation of
parameter importance \cite{Zenke17}. Replay-based approaches
store a small subset of previous samples and constrain the updates with
them \cite{lopezpaz2017gem}, and their distillation variants retain the soft
outputs of the model on previous tasks \cite{buzzega2020dark, Li18}. The
distinction between task-incremental, domain-incremental and
class-incremental scenarios \cite{Ven22} is central for the present study,
because strategies that work under a task oracle do not transfer
automatically to settings where the task identity is unknown at inference
time. Parameter-space regularization has been examined for quantum circuits
\cite{martinperez2026derpp}, while the initialization of the model, which is
the variable manipulated here, has received less attention in that setting.

\subsection{Quantum transfer learning and quantum continual learning}

Hybrid classical-quantum transfer learning freezes a pre-trained classical
network and trains a variational circuit on its features,
\cite{mari2020transfer}. Extensions of this template have characterized the
effect of device noise \cite{martinperez2026cmes} and the behavior of
different classical backbones \cite{martinperez2026soco}. Trained
variational circuits are known to suffer from trainability problems, from
barren plateaus in deep circuits \cite{mcclean2018barren, cerezo2021cost,
Sharma22} to noise-induced flattening of the landscape
\cite{Wang21}, and architectures with local connectivity have been
shown to avoid part of these effects \cite{pesah2021absence}. The continual
case is more recent. A regularization-based quantum continual learning
protocol has been reported on a superconducting processor, where an
elastic-weight-consolidation penalty allowed a quantum classifier to retain
knowledge across three sequential tasks \cite{Zhang26}. Earlier work
described unitary update rules for quantum continual learning in simulation
\cite{senicourt2021quantum}. Across these studies, the pre-training domain
is treated as given, and the question of how the initialization itself
shapes sequential retention is not isolated.

\subsection{Research gaps}

penalty. No prior study isolates the effect of a synthetic cross-domain

\section{Background and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:background}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Variational quantum classifiers}

A variational quantum circuit prepares a state $\ket{\psi(x,\theta)}$ by
applying an encoding unitary that depends on the input $x$ followed by a
parameterised unitary $U(\theta)$, and produces predictions from the
expectation values of a set of observables. Training minimizes a classical
loss with respect to $\theta$ through the parameter-shift rule
\cite{Mitarai18} or through differentiable simulation. The
expressibility and the entangling capability of the parameterised unitary
depend on its topology \cite{sim2019expressibility, grant2018hierarchical},
and the trainability of the circuit depends on the same design choices
\cite{mcclean2018barren, pesah2021absence}.

\subsection{Continual learning scenarios and metrics}

A model that faces a sequence of tasks $\{\mathcal{T}_1, \dots, \mathcal{T}_T\}$
observes the training data of task $t$ while training on task $t$ and, in the
strictest form of the problem, keeps no access to the data of previous
tasks. Two scenarios are distinguished after van de Ven et
al.~\cite{Ven22}. In task-incremental learning the task identity is known at
test time and a shared head evaluates the corresponding decision boundary.
In class-incremental learning the task identity is unknown and a single
output layer must separate classes drawn from every task seen so far. The
present study evaluates the task-incremental scenario and the analysis of
class-incremental behavior is left to the extended protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The evaluation uses the standard continual-learning metrics
\cite{lopezpaz2017gem, Lange22}. Let $A_{t,j}$ be the test accuracy on task
$j$ measured after training through task $t$. The average accuracy and the
average forgetting over the sequence are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{AA} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T} A_{T,j}, \qquad
  \mathrm{AF} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{j=1}^{T-1} \bigl(\max_{t \leq T} A_{t,j} - A_{T,j}\bigr),
  \label{eq:aa_af}
\end{equation}
and the backward transfer is the signed change of each task accuracy between
its completion and the end of the sequence,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BWT} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{j=1}^{T-1} \bigl(A_{T,j} - A_{j,j}\bigr).
  \label{eq:bwt}
\end{equation}
For the two-task protocol analyzed here, the quantity of interest is the
forgetting drop on the first task
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_A = \mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}} - \mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}},
  \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}$ is the accuracy on Task A measured at
the end of the Task A phase and $\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}}$ is the same
accuracy measured after the Task B phase, on the frozen Task A test set. The
relative retention $r_A = \mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}} /
\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}$ is reported alongside $\Delta_A$, because the
absolute drop alone rewards classifiers that never learned the first task.

Two properties fix the role of $\Delta_A$ among these quantities. First, for
a sequence of two tasks the peak accuracy of Task A is attained at the end of
its own phase, so the average forgetting of Eq.~(\ref{eq:aa_af}) and the
backward transfer of Eq.~(\ref{eq:bwt}) reduce to
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_A = \mathrm{AF} = -\mathrm{BWT} \qquad (T = 2),
  \label{eq:delta_identity}
\end{equation}
and the three measures carry the same information at this sequence length.
$\Delta_A$ is used because it is the quantity reported by the earlier
conference version of this study, and it embeds directly into the
$\mathrm{BWT}$ and $\mathrm{AF}$ definitions as the sequence grows. Second,
$\Delta_A$ is bounded above by $\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}$, so a
classifier that never acquires Task A cannot lose much and exhibits a small
drop for the wrong reason. The drop is therefore read jointly with
$\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}$ and $r_A$, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} exhibits this failure mode in the topology
comparison, where the least accurate architecture shows the smallest drop.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Cross-domain synthetic pre-training}

The methodology separates three domains. The source domain $\mathcal{S}$ is
a synthetic binary classification problem generated in four dimensions from
two Gaussian clusters, with no semantic relation to the target tasks. The
sequential domain contains Task A, a binary classification problem built
from two classes of Fashion-MNIST \cite{Xiao17}, and Task B, a binary
problem built from two classes of MNIST \cite{Lecun98}. A hybrid model
consists of a frozen feature extractor and a variational circuit head. For
image tasks, a MobileNetV2 network pre-trained on ImageNet produces a
feature vector that is reduced to four components by principal component
analysis \cite{Sandler18, pearson1901lines}. For the synthetic source the
four features are drawn directly from the Gaussian generator. All features
are rescaled to the interval $[0, \pi]$ to match the rotation domain of the
encoding layer.

Pre-training minimises the cross-entropy loss of the hybrid model on the
synthetic source for a fixed number of epochs and stores the resulting
parameters $\theta_0$. The sequential phase initializes the circuit with
$\theta_0$ and trains on Task A and then on Task B, as summarised in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:protocol}. Two initialization references complete the
design: a scratch model, initialized at random, and a cross-domain model
whose source features come from CIFAR-10 images passed through the same
MobileNetV2 backbone \cite{krizhevsky2009cifar}, which separates the effect
of the synthetic source from the effect of pre-training on real images.

\paragraph{Working hypothesis.} The study tests the hypothesis that
pre-training a parameterised circuit on generic synthetic data yields a more
favourable initialization for subsequent sequential training than a random
draw. Three arguments motivate the expectation. From the classical side,
features learned on a source with no semantic relation to the target still
transfer to it \cite{Yosinski14, pan2009survey}, so the benefit of
pre-training need not depend on domain overlap. From the quantum side, the
initialization of a variational circuit is not neutral for its trainability:
layerwise initialization stabilises the training of small circuits
\cite{Skolik21}, and random draws in wide circuits concentrate in flat
regions of the landscape \cite{mcclean2018barren}. From the design side, an
isotropic Gaussian source with full-rank covariance activates every rotation
axis of the encoding layer, so the source optimization moves the parameters
through a region where the circuit responds to structured inputs, instead of
leaving them near the small-angle regime of a canonical random draw. The
hypothesis yields three testable predictions: the prior reduces the
forgetting drop without raising the accuracy ceiling of the target task. The
gradient signal of the target loss at the pre-trained point is stronger than
at random points, and the benefit does not require semantic overlap between
source and target. The first two are tested in
Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:mechanism}, and the third is probed
by the cross-domain image reference of Experiment~3.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\small
\caption{Cross-domain sequential protocol with synthetic pre-training.}
\label{alg:protocol}
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\Input{Source data $D_S$, tasks $\{D_A, D_B\}$, learning rates $\eta_S, \eta_A, \eta_B$, epochs $E_S, E_A, E_B$}
\Output{$\theta_0$, accuracies $\{\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}, \mathrm{Acc}_B, \mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}}\}$, forgetting drop $\Delta_A$}
Sample $\theta$ at random or load a pre-trained initialization \;
\If{pre-training}{
  Train the hybrid model on $D_S$ with learning rate $\eta_S$ for $E_S$ epochs \;
  Store $\theta_0 \leftarrow \theta$ \;}
Train the model with initialization $\theta$ on $D_A$ with $\eta_A$ for $E_A$ epochs \;
Evaluate $\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}$ on the Task A test set \;
Train the same model on $D_B$ with $\eta_B$ for $E_B$ epochs \;
Evaluate $\mathrm{Acc}_B$ on the Task B test set and $\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}}$ on the Task A test set \;
Return $\Delta_A = \mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}} - \mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}}$ \;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Circuit architectures}

Three topologies are evaluated on four qubits, all preceded by the same
encoding block and followed by the same readout. The encoding applies a
single-qubit rotation around the $X$ axis to each wire,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\psi_{\text{in}}(x)} = \bigotimes_{i=0}^{n-1} R_X(x_i)\ket{0},
  \label{eq:encoding}
\end{equation}
where $x \in [0,\pi]^{n}$ and $n=4$. The readout measures the expectation of
the Pauli-$Z$ operator on the first two wires,
\begin{equation}
  z_k = \braket{\psi_{\text{out}}}{Z_k}{\psi_{\text{out}}}, \quad k \in \{0, 1\},
  \label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
with $\ket{\psi_{\text{out}}} = U(\theta)\ket{\psi_{\text{in}}(x)}$. The two
expectation values feed a fully connected classical layer that produces the
binary logits $\hat{y} = Wz + b$, with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$ and
$b \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$, and the model is trained with categorical
cross-entropy.

\emph{Strongly entangling layers (SEL).} Each layer applies a general
single-qubit rotation $R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ to every wire, followed by a
shifted ring of CNOT gates whose range depends on the layer index,
\begin{equation}
  U_{\text{SEL}}(\theta) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L}
  \Bigl[\prod_{q=0}^{n-1} R_q(\theta_{\ell,q,1}, \theta_{\ell,q,2}, \theta_{\ell,q,3}) \cdot
  \prod_{q=0}^{n-1} \mathrm{CNOT}\bigl(q, (q + r_\ell) \bmod n\bigr)\Bigr],
  \label{eq:sel}
\end{equation}
where $r_\ell = (\ell - 1) \bmod (n-1) + 1$ and the parameter tensor has
shape $(L, n, 3)$, which gives $36$ parameters for the configuration used
here.

\emph{Basic entangler (BE).} Each layer applies one rotation around the $X$
axis per wire, followed by a ring of CNOT gates,
\begin{equation}
  U_{\text{BE}}(\theta) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L}
  \Bigl[\prod_{q=0}^{n-1} R_X(\theta_{\ell,q}) \cdot
  \prod_{q=0}^{n-1} \mathrm{CNOT}\bigl(q, (q+1) \bmod n\bigr)\Bigr],
  \label{eq:be}
\end{equation}
with a parameter tensor of shape $(L, n)$, which gives $12$ parameters.

\emph{Tree tensor network (TTN).} The tree pairs its wires hierarchically.
At the first level, adjacent wires are paired and the information of each
pair accumulates on the right wire, and the procedure repeats on the
surviving wires until a single wire remains. Each block applies one
rotation around $Y$ and one rotation around $Z$ followed by a CNOT,
\begin{equation}
  U_{\text{TTN}}(\theta) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \prod_{b=0}^{n-2}
  \mathrm{CNOT}(i_b \to j_b)\, R_Z(\theta_{\ell,b,2})_{j_b}\, R_Y(\theta_{\ell,b,1})_{i_b},
  \label{eq:ttn}
\end{equation}
where $(i_b, j_b)$ is the wire pair of block $b$ in the hierarchical wiring.
The parameter tensor has shape $(L, n-1, 2)$, which gives $18$ parameters for
$n=4$ and $L=3$. Two properties of this construction are relevant for the
reading of the results. First, the accumulation of information proceeds
towards the right end of the tree, so the subset of parameters that feeds the
first two wires, where the readout of Eq.~(\ref{eq:readout}) is placed, is
smaller than the nominal parameter count. Second, the blocks that act on
subtrees disconnected from the readout wires contribute no gradient under
this readout configuration, an effect measured in the mechanism analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. The comparison across topologies is therefore
also a comparison across effective circuit sizes, and it is reported as such.

\subsection{Sequential training protocol}

The sequential protocol trains a fresh model per configuration and seed. The
pre-training phase runs for fifteen epochs on the source data. The Task A
phase loads the pre-trained parameters $\theta_0$ or a random initialization
and trains on Task A for ten epochs, after which
$\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{init}}$ is evaluated. The Task B phase continues the
training on Task B for ten further epochs with the same optimizer state, and
the protocol ends with the evaluation of $\mathrm{Acc}_B$ and
$\mathrm{Acc}_A^{\text{final}}$. No replay buffer and no parameter-space
penalty are used in the reference protocol. The learning rate differs across
phases and conditions by design of the original campaign, and the
implications of that design choice are discussed with the results.

\subsection{Noise profiles}

Every configuration is replicated under three noise profiles. The ideal
profile propagates the circuit through the state-vector simulator. The two
noisy profiles insert a single-qubit depolarising channel after every
parameterised rotation, a two-qubit depolarising channel after every two-qubit gate, and a bit-flip channel on every measured wire to
emulate readout error. The device profile borrows its parameters from the
median calibration of the IBM Heron r2 generation, with a single-qubit
depolarising rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, a two-qubit rate of $3\times10^{-3}$
and a readout error of $1.5\times10^{-2}$. The legacy profile scales those
rates towards earlier device generations, with a single-qubit rate of
$10^{-3}$, a two-qubit rate of $1.5\times10^{-2}$ and a readout error of
$3\times10^{-2}$.

\subsection{Statistical protocol}

Every configuration is executed with five independent random seeds and every
reported quantity is the mean over seeds with its standard deviation.
Comparisons between two configurations are paired by seed, which removes the
seed-to-seed variance of the shared pipeline from the contrast. Each paired
comparison reports the mean difference, a two-sided paired $t$-test, a
Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a distribution-free check, the Cohen $d_z$
effect size and a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval of the mean difference
estimated with twenty thousand resamples of the seed pairs. Families of
comparisons are corrected with the Holm procedure. The analysis code and the
per-seed payloads are stored with the repository described in the data
availability statement. The reported Wilcoxon probabilities illustrate a
known limitation of five-seed campaigns, namely that the smallest attainable
two-sided $p$-value of the signed-rank test with five pairs is $0.0625$,
which is why the paired $t$-test and the bootstrap interval carry the
inferential weight here and why the extended campaign of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} raises the seed count.

\subsection{Implementation and resources}

The pipeline is implemented in Python 3.11 with PennyLane 0.44.1 for the
quantum simulation, PyTorch 2.10.0 for the hybrid model and the optimizer,
scikit-learn 1.8.0 for the feature reduction, and NumPy 2.4.3. The ideal
profile runs on the state-vector device with the adjoint differentiation
method and the noisy profiles run on the density-matrix device with
backpropagation. The optimizer is Adam with a batch size of 32 and a
learning rate of 0.05 during pre-training. The five-seed campaign of the
conference analysis run on the Hercules cluster of the Centro de
Inform\'atica Cient\'ifica de Andaluc\'ia, on standard nodes with four CPU
cores and eight gigabytes of memory, as independent jobs of SLURM arrays.
The controlled re-run of Experiment~1 reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} was executed outside that cluster, because the
cluster's access VPN was unavailable at run time, on two local workstations:
an Intel Core i5-8500 with six cores and an Intel Core i7-5820K with six
cores and twelve hardware threads, with one single-threaded worker per
run. The software stack of the controlled re-run is Python 3.12.3, PyTorch
2.14.0, PennyLane 0.45.1, scikit-learn 1.9.1 and NumPy 2.5.3, identical on
both machines. Every stored run records the machine identity of the node
that executed it (\texttt{hostname} together with the OS machine
identifier), never a placeholder, so the per-node provenance of each
measurement is auditable from the released payloads. The complete
five-seed campaign over the three noise profiles consists of forty-five
sequential trainings per experiment. The classical front-end of the image sources, namely the
MobileNetV2 feature extraction and the principal-component reduction, is
accounted separately as the reproduction cost of the data preparation and
not as part of the quantum training loop. The end-to-end balance of the
pipeline returns in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, where the scope of the
claims is delimited.

\subsection{Experiment 1: does the synthetic prior reduce the drop?}

Table~\ref{tab:exp1} reports the forgetting drop on Task A for the random
initialization and for the synthetic prior across the three noise profiles,
with the paired reduction and its bootstrap interval. Under the device
profile, the random initialization loses $72.90 \pm 2.01$ points of Task A
accuracy after the Task B phase, and the synthetic prior reduces the loss to
$65.10 \pm 3.73$ points, a paired reduction of $7.80$ points whose bootstrap
interval $[4.50, 12.30]$ excludes zero and whose paired test arrives at
$p = 0.028$, which becomes $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.084$ when the three
per-profile tests are corrected as one family. The ideal profile shows a reduction of $7.00$ points with a
wider interval, and the legacy profile shows the largest nominal reduction,
$9.30$ points, with the widest spread, which reflects the larger seed
variance of the noisiest condition. When the fifteen seed pairs of the three
profiles are pooled, the reduction is $8.03$ points with a bootstrap interval
of $[4.47, 11.63]$, a paired test below $0.001$, a signed-rank test at
$0.003$ and an effect size of $d_z = 1.09$. Read profile by profile, the
device contrast is nominally significant but does not survive the
family-wise correction over the three noise profiles, so the confirmatory
evidence for the sign of the effect is the pooled analysis, and the
per-profile values delineate its robustness across noise conditions.

The absolute magnitude of the effect deserves a precise statement. The

\subsection{Experiment 1 (controlled re-run): matched rates and

Table~\ref{tab:exp1_contrasts} decomposes the headline reduction of
Table~\ref{tab:exp1} into its two factors. The learning-rate regime
carries most of the effect: at matched initialization, the gentler pair
reduces the drop by $5.21$ points from the scratch initialization

$p_{\text{Holm}} < 0.001$, $d_z = -0.69$) and by $5.05$ points from the

analysis of the forty seed pairs. The initialization effect observed at
matched rates is small and does not separate from zero: reductions of
$1.19$ points at the single rate ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.309$) and $1.02$
points at the gentle pair ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.345$), with the same
reading under each noise profile taken separately. The headline
contrast between the pre-trained arm of the conference protocol and its
scratch reference, $6.24$ points with $p_{\text{Holm}} < 0.001$ and
$d_z = -1.10$, is therefore compatible with an effect of the
optimization regime rather than of the prior, and the cross contrast
reinforces this reading: the synthetic arm trained at the single rate
forgets $4.03$ points more than the scratch arm trained at the gentle
pair ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.004$), so the regime dominates the
initialization even when the two factors are pitted against each other.

Table~\ref{tab:exp1_baselines} places the synthetic prior against the
continual-learning baselines, and on retention the contrast is not
favourable to the prior. Relative to the plain scratch arm, the mildest
consolidation reduces forgetting by $9.47$ points and the replay-based
methods by $58.19$ (replay) and $66.49$ points (Dark Experience
Replay++), with $p_{\text{Holm}} < 0.001$ for every contrast and
$|d_z|$ between $1.03$ and $16.79$, while the synthetic-prior arm
forgets $3.24$ points more than the mildest consolidation
($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.023$) and between $52$ and $60$ points more than
the replay-based methods. These reductions are bought with plasticity
rather than obtained for free. The strictest consolidation essentially
blocks the second task: it ends the Task~B phase with $91.08$ to $93.30$
points on Task~A but only $31.55$ to $34.12$ points on Task~B, against
the $87.8$ to $90.4$ points reached by the plain and synthetic arms.
Replay and Dark Experience Replay++ occupy intermediate positions on
Task~B ($73.15$ and $61.12$ points under the ideal profile), so the
table describes a stability-plasticity frontier rather than a ranking
on $\Delta_A$ alone.

The controlled evidence therefore narrows the claim that the conference
version attached to Table~\ref{tab:exp1}: at this scale and with
matched rates, the cross-domain prior contributes a small and
non-significant reduction of forgetting under both noise conditions,
the regime of the sequential phases contributes a significant one, and
the regularization and rehearsal baselines absent from the conference
version dominate retention at the explicit price of Task~B plasticity.

\subsection{Experiment 2: topology capacity and retention}

\subsection{Scenario axis: task-IL and class-IL benchmarks}

\subsection{Regularization family and interaction with the synthetic prior}
\label{sec:e6_family}

The regularization family closes the comparison on the standard benchmarks.
Table~\ref{tab:e6_family} reports elastic weight consolidation at the
campaign strength, uniform L2 drift and synaptic intelligence at two
penalty strengths each, and the two memory-based arms, all under the same
protocol. Synaptic intelligence at the lower strength is the only
weight-regularization baseline that improves average accuracy over the
plain sequential model, with a paired difference of $+4.07$ points
($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.007$), and it does so while reducing average
forgetting from $77.4$ to $6.8$ points, below every memory-based remedy in
the comparison. At the higher strength the same algorithm freezes the
model after the first task, which shows the sensitivity of the family to
the penalty calibration. Elastic weight consolidation and the uniform L2
penalty do not beat the plain baseline at either strength, with paired
differences between $-1.7$ and $-6.7$ points. Dark experience replay
reproduces the pattern of the two-task protocol on the five-task
benchmark, with a paired gain of $+11.78$ points ($p_{\text{Holm}} <
0.001$) behind rehearsal ($+15.49$ points) and ahead of every
regularization arm.

\subsection{Longer task chains}
\label{sec:longer_chains}

The scenario and benchmark campaigns were complemented with ten-task chains, one class per
task, on split-MNIST and split-Fashion-MNIST, to test whether the ranking of strategies
survives longer sequences. Table~\ref{tab:e7_chains} reports the average accuracy and
average forgetting of the same arms under the same protocol. The plain model collapses to
the ten-class chance level, with a forgetting of $89.99$ points, and the synthetic prior
does not change it, with a paired difference of $+0.03$ points. The memory-based arms keep
a significant advantage. Rehearsal gains $+5.68$ points and dark experience replay $+8.45$
points on split-MNIST (Holm-corrected $p < 0.001$ in both cases), and both gains reproduce
on split-Fashion-MNIST at $+7.28$ and $+8.77$ points. Synaptic intelligence preserves its
low-forgetting profile, $8.35$ points against $89.99$ of the plain model, but its accuracy
advantage dilutes without significance, $+1.08$ points at the chosen strength and $+0.66$
and $+0.76$ points at the weaker ones, while elastic weight consolidation stays at the
plain level at both tested strengths, $ -0.06$ and $-0.00$ points. The hyperparameter
probes of the same campaign confirm the insensitivity of the family. Doubling the rehearsal
buffer from twenty-five to fifty percent changes nothing measurable, $+0.22$ points against
the smaller buffer, and the elastic-weight penalty does not recover its deficit either, as
it did not on the five-task benchmarks. Longer chains therefore sharpen the reading of the
previous sections. The memory-free remedies do not carry retention when interference
accumulates over ten tasks, and the arms with an explicit memory are the only components
that improve it.

\subsection{Mechanism analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The mechanism section combines the exploratory gradient probes of the
review packet with the four checkpoint-based measurements of the extended
protocol: per-layer gradient variance, empirical Fisher spectra, the loss
barrier between the two task optima, and the parameter distributions
including angle wrapping. All measurements use first-order autograd or
double-precision finite differences with an explicit self-test, never
second-order autodiff through the PennyLane layer, whose unreliability in
this stack was documented during the audit (caveat T1). The checkpoints
were re-trained locally for ten seeds per arm with the exact two-task
protocol of the campaign (four-qubit strongly entangling ansatz, matched
rates).

The gradient-variance probe of the review packet sweeps the barren-plateau
proxy over qubit counts from four to twelve and both circuit topologies:
the variance of the cost gradient decays from $5.2\times10^{-3}$ to
$1.2\times10^{-5}$ for the strongly entangling ansatz between four and
twelve qubits, while the hierarchical wiring decays far more gently, from
$6.8\times10^{-3}$ to $3.6\times10^{-4}$, keeping about thirty times
more gradient variance at twelve qubits
\cite{mcclean2018barren,pesah2021absence}. The checkpoint measurements
sharpen this into a per-layer statement at the initialization the two arms
actually use. At $\theta_0$, the pre-trained point, the per-layer gradient
variance of the Task-A loss is
2.80e+00, 3.67e+00
and 2.09e+00 for the three ansatz layers
(mean over layers 2.85e+00), against
2.73e-03, 3.03e-03
and 1.36e-03 at random initialization
(2.37e-03), a gap of three orders of magnitude,
uniformly across the layers, which means the source optimization lands the
circuit in an active, high-gradient region of the landscape rather than the
weak-gradient region that random rotation angles occupy
(Figure~\ref{fig:e5}a). After each arm has learned the first task the two
checkpoints collapse onto comparably weak gradients
(6.18e-02 vs\ 1.71e-01), and by the end
of the second task both models exhibit renewed large gradients on Task-A
data (1.15e+00 vs\ 2.12e+00), the
signature of the interference that the accuracy matrices quantify.

The empirical Fisher spectrum at $\theta_0$ tells the same story in terms
of curvature: the trace is 1.13e+00 at random
initialization against 1.27e+02 at the prior
point, with a fuller spectrum (spectral entropy
1.05 vs\ 1.43
and effective rank 2.9 vs\ 4.2). The top eigenvalue carries about half of the trace in both cases
(Figure~\ref{fig:e5}b). The interpolation between the two task optima
is the measurement that does not separate the arms: the linear path from
$\theta_A$ to $\theta_B$ shows no loss barrier on the newly learned task
for either arm (barrier height +0.000 and
+0.000. The two task solutions are linearly
connected in this parametrisation), and the parameter distance travelled
is essentially identical
($\|\theta_B-\theta_A\|/\sqrt{d} = 1.040$ vs.\ $1.063$).
Forgetting in this model is therefore not a barrier phenomenon between the
two solutions. What separates the arms is where the optimization starts
relative to both of them (Figure~\ref{fig:e5}c).

Finally, the parameter distributions close an open item of the preliminary
analysis: the circuit is $2\pi$-periodic in each rotation angle, which was
verified numerically: wrapping the 36 angles of the pre-trained circuit
reproduces the loss to floating-point precision
(maximum $|\Delta\mathcal{L}|$ = 3.3e-06).
Random initialization covers exactly one period
(uniform in $[0, 2\pi)$), while the source phase pushes some coordinates
beyond it (raw range up to about 7.6
radians). After wrapping, the effective distributions differ mainly in
dispersion (standard deviation 1.74 for random
initialization against 2.01 for the prior, with
42\% and
52\% of the raw angles outside the
canonical interval). The gradient and curvature evidence above is the part
with decision weight. The distributional description documents the
effective working point of the pre-trained circuit. Self-tests: the
finite-difference check of the gradient path agrees at
6e-08 relative error,
and the two ways of computing the Fisher trace agree at
2e-05.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ======================================================================

The results support a precise and bounded reading of synthetic cross-domain
pre-training. The controlled factorial re-run redistributes the effect that
the original protocol attributed to the initialization. The apparent
reduction of the forgetting drop, of about six points in the uncontrolled
comparison, is carried by the learning-rate regime, which accounts for
about five points under matched schedules, while the synthetic
initialization produces no significant change once the rates are matched.
The benchmark campaign extends the null reading beyond the original
scenario. The prior does not separate from a random initialization on the
class-incremental sequences of up to five tasks, on the split benchmarks of
MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, along the data budgets from five hundred to twelve
thousand samples, or on the scaling grid over qubit counts and circuit
depths. The study therefore answers its motivating question with a
controlled null result at this scale, supported by an explicit statistical
protocol and a full record of the per-seed payloads.

The mechanism evidence adds a second result. The pre-trained point is not a
minimum of the target task and not a better starting region in terms of
retention, yet its gradient signal is roughly three orders of magnitude
stronger in the first layer than the signal available at a random
initialization, and its empirical Fisher spectrum is about two orders of
magnitude larger. The loss interpolation between the two task optima shows
no barrier along the linear path, so the separation between the arms lies
in the starting point rather than in the connectivity of the landscape.
Taken together, the probes dissociate gradient signal from
continual-learning performance at this scale, since a starting point with
far more active gradients does not retain earlier tasks better. This
dissociation is informative for the design of memory-free strategies
because it removes gradient strength from the list of candidate mechanisms
that alone can explain retention.

The component comparison completes the picture in the direction of
practice. On the standard class-incremental benchmarks, rehearsal yields the
largest benefit, with an advantage of about fifteen points over a plain
sequential baseline, at a quantified cost in plasticity, while elastic
weight consolidation trades most of its plasticity for the retention
gains it obtains. Among the weight-regularization baselines, synaptic
intelligence is the only one that improves over the plain model, with a
modest gain of about four points and a tenfold reduction of forgetting,
which places the regularization family behind the memory-based remedies
in this comparison. The synthetic prior, by
contrast, behaves as a neutral initialization under this protocol. For
practitioners, the operational reading is that memory-free pre-training
can be used without harm but should not be expected to carry retention,
and that claims of transfer effects in quantum continual learning should
be established under a matched optimization regime before attribution.

Three constraints delimit the scope of the claims. The first is scale. The
headline experiments use four qubits while the scaling grid reaches eight,
the sequences contain up to five tasks, and the noise model, while
calibrated on a current device generation, is simulated rather than
executed on hardware. The second is the evaluation scenario. The extended
protocol now covers the class-incremental setting of the classical
benchmarks \cite{Ven22} alongside the task-incremental setting of the
original protocol, and the null reading holds in both. The third is the
classical share of the pipeline. The MobileNetV2 feature extraction and
the principal-component reduction dominate the wall-clock cost of the image
sources, which bounds any practical advantage that the quantum head could
claim in the current configuration.

Several directions follow from these limits. A validation on physical
hardware would test whether the dissociation between gradient strength and
retention survives realistic noise correlations beyond the calibrated
model. Longer task sequences and stronger feature spaces would probe
whether the null reading persists as interference accumulates further,
which the present budgets begin to sketch. Meta-learned initializations,
which optimize the starting point for sequential adaptation rather than
for a single source task, are a natural candidate for a strategy that acts
where the mechanism probes look, and their evaluation under this protocol
is left for future work.

% ======================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% ======================================================================

This study characterized cross-domain synthetic pre-training as a
memory-free initialization for sequential quantum classifiers through a
controlled factorial design and a benchmark campaign of 650 training runs.
The controlled evidence relocates the effect that the original protocol
attributed to the initialization. Under matched learning rates the
synthetic prior produces no significant change in forgetting, while the
optimization regime carries a reduction of about five points, and the null
reading of the initialization is stable across class-incremental
benchmarks of up to five tasks, qubit counts from four to eight and data
budgets from five hundred to twelve thousand samples. The corrected
reading of the topology comparison further shows that the apparent
retention advantage of the constrained architecture reflects its limited
capacity to learn the first task. The mechanism probes show that the prior
places the circuit in a much more active gradient region without a
corresponding gain in retention, so gradient signal and continual-learning
performance dissociate at this scale. Among the evaluated remedies,
rehearsal is the component with the largest measured benefit on the
standard benchmarks, at a quantified cost in plasticity, and synaptic
intelligence is the only weight-regularization baseline that improves
over the plain model while reducing forgetting by an order of magnitude. The evidence is
limited to simulated devices and circuits of at most eight qubits, and the
classical front-end cost of the pipeline is declared explicitly. The
practical reading is twofold: claims of transfer effects in quantum
continual learning must be established under matched optimization regimes,
and memory-free initialization alone does not remove forgetting at this
scale.

\section{Circuit resources and fault-tolerant estimate}
\label{app:resources}

Table~\ref{tab:e4_resources} lists the gate-level resources of the trained
ansatz for the qubit counts and depths of the scaling grid. Every rotation
gate of the circuit is a general single-qubit rotation. Under the standard
synthesis cost model of Ross and Selinger \cite{ross2016optimal} an
arbitrary $z$-rotation approximated to error $\varepsilon$ costs about
$3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ $T$ gates, and no generic single-qubit gate can be
synthesised below three $T$ gates \cite{bocharov2012resource}. At
$\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ the estimate is about
60 $T$ gates per rotation,
i.e.\ about 2392 $T$ for the four-qubit
three-layer circuit and 4784 $T$ at eight
qubits. CNOTs are Clifford operations and contribute no $T$ gates. The
estimate counts every Euler angle of each rotation gate separately and is
therefore conservative at the gate level. It excludes state preparation,
measurement, and error-correction overheads, and it is reported as an
order-of-magnitude reality check on what the demonstrated scale implies for
the fault-tolerant era, not as a compilation study. At twelve qubits and
four layers the same accounting reaches 9328
$T$ gates per inference, which is the quantitative form of the
resource-awareness argument in the introduction.
